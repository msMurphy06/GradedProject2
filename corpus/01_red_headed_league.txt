The Red-Headed League

Mr Jabez Wilson is a pawnbroker who runs a small shop near Coburg Square in London. He is a stout, florid man, and his most notable feature is his head of brilliantly red hair. Wilson presents himself at 221B Baker Street one autumn morning to consult Sherlock Holmes and Doctor Watson. He carries with him a clipped newspaper advertisement and a sheet of paper, and he is at first reluctant to tell his story, fearing he will be laughed at.

The Vacancy in the Red-Headed League

Eight weeks before his visit, Wilson explains, he was shown the advertisement by his assistant, a young man named Vincent Spaulding. The notice announced a vacancy in the Red-Headed League, a society founded by the will of one Ezekiah Hopkins of Lebanon, Pennsylvania. The League existed solely to provide easy work at four pounds a week to red-headed men of sound body and mind. Spaulding urged Wilson to apply, pointing out how few men had hair as red as his.

Wilson presented himself at the league's small office in Pope's Court, off Fleet Street. The room was crowded with red-headed applicants — men of every shade of red, from straw to dark mahogany. The interview was conducted by a Mr Duncan Ross, who selected Wilson immediately on the strength of his unusually vivid colour, even pulling Wilson's hair to confirm it was genuine.

The Strange Work

Wilson's duty was strange in the extreme. Each morning from ten o'clock to two, he was to sit in the league's office and copy out the Encyclopaedia Britannica in longhand. He was not permitted to leave the office during these hours on any account, and the work paid four pounds weekly. The young assistant, Vincent Spaulding, would manage the pawnshop in his absence. For eight weeks Wilson copied the encyclopaedia faithfully, working his way through letters A and partway into B. He had been told the work might continue for years.

On the morning Wilson came to consult Holmes, he had found the office locked. A card pinned to the door read simply: "The Red-Headed League is dissolved. October 9, 1890." Inquiries with the landlord revealed that no one of the name Duncan Ross had ever rented the office; the man had described himself as a solicitor and used the office only as a temporary expedient.

Holmes Investigates

Holmes asks Wilson about his pawnshop assistant. Spaulding, Wilson says, is small, quick, and clever, willing to work for half wages because he wishes to learn the trade. His only oddity is a fondness for photography, and he spends much time in the cellar developing pictures. Holmes notes that Spaulding has been with Wilson for about a month before the Red-Headed League advertisement appeared.

After Wilson leaves, Holmes and Watson visit Coburg Square. Holmes thumps the pavement in front of Wilson's shop with his stick. He rings the bell and, when Spaulding answers, asks for directions to the Strand, observing the man's trousers carefully. Holmes then walks around the block and points out to Watson that the back of Wilson's shop adjoins the City and Suburban Bank's Coburg branch. The bank, Holmes notes to Watson, recently received a shipment of thirty thousand napoleons in gold from the Bank of France.

The Identity of the Assistant

Holmes recognises Spaulding as John Clay, the grandson of a royal duke and one of the most cunning thieves in London. Clay is known to be wanted for murder, theft, smashing and forgery, and his trademark is the white patches on his trouser knees — the result of long hours digging in confined spaces. Holmes has been waiting for Clay to surface.

The League, Holmes deduces, was an elaborate device to get Wilson out of his shop for four hours each day. With Wilson absent and Spaulding in charge below stairs, Clay had been tunnelling from Wilson's cellar to the vault of the City and Suburban Bank. The dissolution of the league signalled that the tunnel was complete.

The Capture

That night, Holmes, Watson, the police inspector Peter Jones, and Mr Merryweather (a director of the bank) wait in darkness in the bank's cellar. After a long silence, a flagstone shifts and Clay's head appears, followed by his confederate Archie. Jones seizes Clay by the collar. Clay is unsurprised, accepts the arrest with a sneer of aristocratic disdain, and asks his captors to say "please" when ordering him about.

In the aftermath, Holmes confesses to Watson that he had suspected the league's purpose from the moment Wilson mentioned Spaulding's frequent disappearances to the cellar to develop photographs. The dirty trousers confirmed the tunnel. The thumping of the pavement told Holmes which direction the tunnel ran. The connection of the shop's cellar to the bank vault made the design obvious.

Wilson received his usual wage for his weeks of copying and lost only his strange employer. The League had paid him thirty-two pounds, all from John Clay's pocket — money Clay had calculated was a small price for unimpeded access to thirty thousand pounds in French gold.

Holmes remarks to Watson that the affair, while not difficult, was original. "It saved me from ennui," he says. The case is dated to October 1890 and was published among Watson's accounts of his friend's earlier investigations.
