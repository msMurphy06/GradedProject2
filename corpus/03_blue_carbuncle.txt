The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle

Two mornings after Christmas, Doctor Watson visits Sherlock Holmes at 221B Baker Street and finds him stretched on the sofa contemplating a very old and battered hard felt hat. The hat lies on a wooden chair, with a lens and forceps beside it, and Holmes is examining it with evident satisfaction. The hat had been brought to him that morning by Peterson, the commissionaire, in connection with a small Christmas-morning incident in Tottenham Court Road.

The Hat and the Goose

On Christmas morning at around four o'clock, Peterson had been walking home through Tottenham Court Road when he saw a tallish man in a black coat carrying a fat goose over his shoulder. A group of roughs accosted the man; in the scuffle, his hat fell off, and as Peterson rushed to assist, the man — startled — dropped the goose and fled. Peterson, in his commissionaire's uniform, was likely mistaken for a constable by both the assailants and the victim.

Peterson rescued the goose and the hat. A small card was attached to the goose's leg, marked: "For Mrs Henry Baker." The initials "H. B." were also faintly inked inside the hatband. Peterson took both objects to Holmes, who agreed to make inquiries. The goose itself, having begun to spoil, was sent home to Peterson with the suggestion that he eat it before it went bad.

The Carbuncle

While Peterson and his wife were preparing the goose for the table, his wife found, embedded in the bird's crop, a brilliant blue gemstone. Peterson rushed back to Baker Street with the discovery. Holmes recognised the stone immediately: it was the Blue Carbuncle of the Countess of Morcar, a remarkable gem stolen five days earlier from the lady's bedroom at the Hotel Cosmopolitan.

The Theft at the Cosmopolitan

The newspapers had been full of the theft. The Countess of Morcar's blue carbuncle, a gem of unusual size and lustre — described as the only blue carbuncle in the world — had been taken from her jewel case while she was lodged at the Cosmopolitan. A reward of one thousand pounds had been offered. James Ryder, an attendant at the hotel, had given evidence at the magistrate's investigation. Suspicion had fallen on John Horner, a plumber who had been called to repair the grate in the Countess's dressing-room on the day of the theft. Horner had a previous conviction for robbery and was already in custody.

Tracing Henry Baker

Holmes places an advertisement in the evening papers, summoning a Mr Henry Baker to call at Baker Street to recover his lost property. A diffident, shabby-looking man calls at the appointed hour. He is grateful for the hat and accepts the substitute goose Holmes has procured, but expresses no curiosity about the original. Holmes is satisfied that Henry Baker had no idea his Christmas goose was anything other than ordinary fare.

Baker explains that he received the goose from the landlord of the Alpha Inn near the British Museum. The Alpha had run a goose-club: members paid a few pence weekly through the year, and at Christmas each member received a goose. Holmes and Watson immediately set out for the Alpha.

The Trail of the Goose

The landlord of the Alpha directs Holmes to Mr Breckinridge, a salesman at Covent Garden Market, from whom he bought the geese. Breckinridge is a quarrelsome man, sick of being asked about geese. Holmes — by a series of leading remarks designed to needle Breckinridge into talking — extracts the information that this lot of geese came from Mrs Oakshott of Brixton Road. Breckinridge has been pestered all day by another man asking about the same birds.

While Holmes and Watson are still at the market, a small ferret-faced man in a long black coat hurries up to Breckinridge. He asks anxiously about the geese, mentions Mrs Oakshott, and is rebuffed angrily. Holmes intercepts him as he turns away.

James Ryder Confesses

The man is James Ryder — the same hotel attendant who had testified against John Horner. Holmes brings Ryder back to Baker Street. Faced with the stone laid out on a saucer before him, Ryder collapses. He confesses everything.

He had taken the stone from the Countess's jewel case with the help of the Countess's maid, Catherine Cusack — laying suspicion on the unfortunate Horner. Fearing a search, Ryder fled to his sister's house in Brixton: Mrs Oakshott, whose husband bred geese. There, in a panic, he opened the bill of one of his sister's geese and pushed the stone down its throat. The bird was one of a number marked for sale at Christmas. By the time Ryder returned to choose his goose, his sister had already sold a bird of identical appearance to her brother — the wrong goose. The right goose had been bought by the Alpha goose-club and given to Henry Baker.

Holmes Lets Him Go

Holmes considers Ryder's wretched, snivelling figure and lets him go. Horner, he reasons, will be released the moment the stone is returned: with no testimony from Ryder, the case against Horner collapses. Prosecuting Ryder would gain nothing for Horner and would only manufacture a confirmed criminal out of a frightened fool. "After all, Watson," Holmes says, "I am not retained by the police to supply their deficiencies."

The blue carbuncle, polished and twinkling on the desk before them, is in Holmes's keeping until the morning, when it will be returned to the Countess.
