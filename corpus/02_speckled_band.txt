The Adventure of the Speckled Band

Helen Stoner, a young woman of thirty wearing a black veil and visibly trembling, arrives at 221B Baker Street in the early hours of an April morning in 1883. She has travelled by dog-cart and the first train from Leatherhead in Surrey, driven by fear. Sherlock Holmes and Doctor Watson receive her in their sitting-room, where Holmes notes the spatters of mud on her travelling cloak and the marks of a recent train journey on her shoes.

Stoke Moran and Doctor Roylott

Helen lives at Stoke Moran, a crumbling old manor house on the western edge of Surrey. Her stepfather is Doctor Grimesby Roylott, the last surviving heir of one of the oldest Saxon families in England — a line that once held vast estates and is now reduced to a few acres and the ancient house. Doctor Roylott had studied medicine and practised in Calcutta. There he had served a long prison term for beating his butler to death in a fit of rage. He returned to England a soured man, married Helen's widowed mother in India, and kept the family with her annuity after her death in a railway accident.

The doctor's habits at Stoke Moran are described as violent and eccentric. He has befriended a band of travelling gipsies, who camp on his grounds. He keeps a cheetah and a baboon, brought from India and allowed to roam freely. He has quarrelled with every neighbour and is feared in the parish. He is a man of immense physical strength, capable of bending a steel poker into a curve with his bare hands.

The Death of Julia Stoner

Helen had a twin sister, Julia, who died two years before Helen's visit to Baker Street. At the time of her death, Julia had been engaged to a half-pay major of marines. In the fortnight before the wedding, Doctor Roylott ordered the house's east wing repaired, and Julia was moved from her own bedroom to the room adjacent to her stepfather's. Several nights before her death, Julia had complained to Helen of hearing a low whistle in the early hours of the morning, and a metallic clang shortly after.

On the night of her death, Julia came to Helen's room visibly agitated. The two sisters spoke briefly; Julia returned to her own bedroom and locked her door from the inside. In the early hours, Helen was awoken by a wild scream. Running to Julia's door, she heard her sister cry out in terror: "Oh, my God! Helen! It was the band! The speckled band!" Julia collapsed in the corridor and died within minutes, conscious only long enough to point and gesture toward her stepfather's room. The coroner could find no cause of death, no poison, no wound, no struggle. The door had been locked from inside and the shutters were closed and barred.

Helen's Predicament

In the two years since Julia's death, Helen had lived alone with Doctor Roylott. She had recently become engaged to a Percy Armitage, second son of Mr Armitage of Crane Water near Reading. Two days before her visit to Holmes, repairs began on the wall of Helen's bedroom, and she was moved to the very room in which Julia had died. The night before her visit, she had heard the same low whistle her sister had described.

Holmes Investigates

Holmes promises to come to Stoke Moran that same day. After Helen leaves, Doctor Roylott himself bursts into the Baker Street sitting-room. He is enormous, his face seared by sun and marked with a thousand wrinkles, and he warns Holmes off the case in a voice of contemptuous fury. He bends the steel poker as a parting demonstration. Holmes calmly bends it straight again after Roylott leaves.

That afternoon, Holmes and Watson travel to Stoke Moran in Helen's absence (Doctor Roylott had gone to town). They examine the three bedrooms in the inhabited wing: Helen's old room, Julia's old room, and Doctor Roylott's room. In Julia's room (now Helen's), Holmes notes several oddities: a bell-rope that connects to nothing — it is a dummy, attached to a ventilator that opens into Doctor Roylott's bedroom rather than the outside air, as ventilators normally would. The bed is bolted to the floor and cannot be moved. The lamp burns near the bed. The dummy bell-pull hangs above the pillow.

In Doctor Roylott's room, Holmes finds a saucer of milk on a chair, a small lash with a loop at one end, and a large iron safe whose top is heavily marked. The safe, Holmes learns, has been in the room some weeks.

The Vigil

Holmes instructs Helen to spend the night with Watson and himself in her sister's old bedroom, while she pretends to retire to her newly-repaired room. After dark, Holmes and Watson station themselves in Julia's room with a lamp ready and Holmes's cane in hand. They wait in silence.

Just after three in the morning, Holmes lights the lamp and lashes furiously at the bell-rope. A wild scream from the next room follows almost immediately. Rushing into Doctor Roylott's bedroom, Holmes and Watson find him dead in his chair, with a yellow band marked with brown spots wrapped about his head. Holmes lifts it cautiously — it is a swamp adder, the deadliest snake in India.

The Explanation

Doctor Roylott had trained the snake to climb the bell-rope through the ventilator and back. He whistled to call it home and rewarded it with milk. On the night of Julia's death, the snake had bitten her as she lay in bed — leaving no mark visible to a coroner because of its tiny puncture and its quick action. The bed was bolted in position so the victim could not be moved out of range. Holmes's blow on the rope had turned the snake back through the ventilator, and it had attacked the only living thing in the room beyond.

Doctor Roylott had killed his stepdaughter Julia for her share of the late mother's annuity, which would have transferred to her on marriage. He had been about to kill Helen for the same reason.

Holmes, in the closing remarks of the case, accepts no blame for Doctor Roylott's death. "I cannot say that it is likely to weigh heavily on my conscience," he tells Watson.
