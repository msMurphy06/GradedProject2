A Scandal in Bohemia

To Sherlock Holmes she is always THE woman. Doctor Watson opens his account of this case with that observation. The events took place in March 1888, shortly after Watson's marriage and his departure from Baker Street. Returning one evening from a professional visit, Watson found himself in Baker Street and dropped in on his old friend Holmes.

The Cipher Note

Holmes shows Watson a note delivered that evening. The paper is unusually thick, watermarked with the letters "Eg P Gt" — meaning, Holmes explains by reference to a continental gazetteer, paper manufactured in the town of Egria in Bohemia. The note announces that a gentleman will call at a quarter to eight that evening on a matter of the utmost importance, masked.

The visitor arrives precisely on time. He is a giant of a man, over six feet six, dressed in a long Astrakhan coat and a black mask. He introduces himself first as Count Von Kramm, then — when Holmes notes that he can hardly act in the dark — admits his true identity: Wilhelm Gottsreich Sigismond von Ormstein, Grand Duke of Cassel-Felstein and hereditary King of Bohemia. The case is so delicate that he has come to London himself rather than entrust it to any subordinate.

The Photograph

Five years earlier, the King, then Crown Prince, had formed an attachment with a celebrated American opera contralto named Irene Adler, born in New Jersey in 1858. They had met in Warsaw. The connection had been brief. The King had since become engaged to Clotilde Lothman von Saxe-Meiningen, the second daughter of the King of Scandinavia — a marriage of state.

The difficulty is a photograph. The photograph shows the King and Irene Adler together, and is unmistakably them, not a forgery. The King had been indiscreet — had perhaps even written compromising letters — but the photograph is the immediate threat. Adler has it in her possession. She has threatened to send it to the bride's family on the very day the betrothal is publicly announced — three days hence — out of revenge for being cast aside.

The King has tried everything. He has had Adler's house — Briony Lodge, Serpentine Avenue, St John's Wood — burgled twice. Her luggage has been intercepted and searched on two railway journeys. She has been waylaid in the street. The photograph has never been found. Holmes agrees to take the case. The King pays a thousand pounds in advance and leaves another hundred in notes against expenses.

Holmes Disguises Himself

The following morning Holmes leaves Baker Street disguised as a drunken, out-of-work groom. He reappears at five o'clock, transformed back into himself, eats a chop, and tells Watson the day's work.

He had loitered around Briony Lodge in his disguise. He had befriended the cab-rank ostlers and learned the household's habits. Irene Adler lives quietly. She has one regular gentleman caller — Mr Godfrey Norton, a lawyer of the Inner Temple — who comes once or sometimes twice daily. That morning Holmes had observed Norton arrive at the house in great agitation, leave again twenty minutes later in a cab calling for "the Church of St Monica in the Edgware Road," and Adler herself follow a few minutes later in a landau giving the same direction.

Holmes had himself called a cab and followed. At St Monica's they were married — by the priest, in the presence of two witnesses, of whom Holmes (entering the church and asked to oblige) became one. He was paid a sovereign for his trouble. The bride and groom drove off in opposite directions afterward, both swearing eternal fidelity. Adler returned to Briony Lodge.

The Smoke and Fire Plan

Holmes plans for that evening. He needs Irene Adler to betray, by her own action, the hiding-place of the photograph. He explains the trick to Watson. Watson is to wait outside Briony Lodge with a smoke rocket. When Holmes — who will engineer a quarrel in the street to be carried into Adler's drawing-room as an injured stranger — gives the signal by raising his hand, Watson is to throw the rocket through the open sitting-room window and shout "Fire!"

A woman's first instinct in a fire, Holmes explains, is to rush to the thing she values most. A bachelor seizes his children, a married woman seizes her jewel case, an unattached woman seizes the most precious of her possessions. In the case of Irene Adler, with no fortune at risk in the house, Holmes is convinced this will be the photograph. He has only to watch where she runs.

The Execution

The plan works exactly as Holmes has predicted. At Briony Lodge that evening, a brawl breaks out among loiterers; Holmes (made up as a clergyman) interposes, is "struck," and is carried bleeding into Adler's house. Inside, in the sitting-room, the smoke rocket comes through the window; cries of fire echo through the building. Irene Adler dashes to a sliding panel above the bell-pull on the right side of the fireplace, takes from it a photograph, then — recovering herself as the false alarm is recognised — replaces it and walks calmly from the room.

Holmes has seen everything. He leaves immediately, returns to Baker Street, and tells Watson he will call on the King the following morning and recover the photograph from its hiding-place in their presence.

Irene Adler Wins

The next morning Holmes and Watson and the King arrive at Briony Lodge. They are admitted by an elderly maid-servant, who tells them with smiling pleasure that her mistress and her new husband left the country by the five-fifteen train from Charing Cross the previous night, bound for the Continent, never to return.

Holmes flings open the sliding panel above the bell-pull. The photograph is gone. In its place is a photograph of Irene Adler alone, in evening dress, and a letter addressed to "Sherlock Holmes, Esq. To be left till called for."

The letter, dated at midnight, is from Adler. She had recognised Holmes in his groom disguise that morning; she had recognised him again in the clergyman who was carried into her house. The fire trick was old; she had read of it. She had followed Holmes back to Baker Street to make absolutely certain. She had then resolved on instant flight with her husband — whom she had married that very afternoon at St Monica's, partly for love and partly to make this evasion final. She had taken the photograph with her, but promised the King she would never use it against him: she had married a man whose love was more to her than the King's hatred. She kept the photograph only as a safeguard in case the King ever tried to harm her or her husband. The photograph of herself in evening dress was, she wrote, a parting gift for "Mr Sherlock Holmes."

The King is astonished and delighted. "Was she not a most admirable adversary?" he exclaims. "What a queen she would have made!" He offers Holmes an emerald snake ring as a token of gratitude. Holmes refuses anything but the photograph of Adler herself, which he keeps. The King's marriage to the Princess Clotilde proceeds without incident.

This is the only case Watson records, in his accounts, where Sherlock Holmes was beaten by the cleverness of a woman. He always speaks of her afterward — without diminution — under the honourable title of THE woman.
